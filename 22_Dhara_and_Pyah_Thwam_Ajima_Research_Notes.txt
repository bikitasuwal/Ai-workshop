HERITAGE RESEARCH NOTES: 22 DHARA (BAISDHARA) AND PYAH THWAM AJIMA
Balaju, Kathmandu | Desk-research summary

NOTE ON ACCURACY
These notes summarize published reporting and academic context. They are not a substitute for consultation with local Guthi members, priests, traditional artists, residents, or heritage authorities. Claims based on belief or oral tradition are identified as such.

============================================================
1. 22 DHARA / BAISDHARA, BALAJU
============================================================

WHAT IT IS
22 Dhara, also known as Baisdhara, is a historic water-spout complex in Balaju, near the Nagarjun forest. It is known for 22 stone water spouts (dhunge dhara) and is part of the wider Balaju park and religious landscape. Local reporting also associates the place with the Newar name Lhuti. Reports describe nearby shrines dedicated to deities including Vishnu and Shiva.

HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Several published reports attribute 21 of the spouts to King Jaya Prakash Malla, commonly placing their construction around Nepal Sambat 875. They report that an additional spout was later added by King Rana Bahadur Shah in 1855 BS.

Caution: This attribution is widely repeated in news reports, but the sources reviewed do not establish it through a primary inscription, archival record, or archaeological study. Present it as the commonly reported account, not as conclusively proven history.

WATER AND TRADITIONAL ENGINEERING
The 22 stone spouts are the defining physical feature. They represent traditional public water infrastructure and are also used for ritual bathing. Important research questions include how the water source, channels, spout structures, drainage, and surrounding landscape have been maintained.

The sources reviewed do not provide a detailed engineering assessment or reliable current water-quality data. Do not claim that the water is safe to drink or has medical properties without testing.

RELIGIOUS AND CULTURAL SIGNIFICANCE
Devotees visit for ritual bathing. Religious belief holds that bathing here can bring spiritual merit, blessings, or purification. Some accounts compare the spiritual value with bathing at pilgrimage sites such as Muktinath and Gosaikunda.

Some reports mention a belief that bathing can protect against or cure skin diseases. This is a traditional belief, not a scientifically established medical treatment. It must not be presented as health advice.

BAISDHARA MELA / LHUTI PUNHI
An annual gathering known as Baisdhara Mela is held at the site. Reports describe devotees bathing at the spouts and participating in religious activities. The event is also referred to in local Newar context as Lhuti Punhi.

Some reporting describes people lighting lamps in memory of deceased relatives. Other accounts connect the wider festival period with local deity traditions, including links with Manmaiju Devi Jatra and Kadelchok Bhagwati.

IMPORTANT DATE CAUTION
Published reports do not consistently state the festival date. Some identify it with Chaitra Shukla Purnima, while an OnlineKhabar report from 2024 describes it as the full moon of Baisakh. Confirm the current year's date with the local festival committee or relevant Guthi before publishing it.

PUBLIC AND HERITAGE VALUE
The site has several overlapping roles:
- Historic public water infrastructure.
- A place of religious bathing and worship.
- A gathering place for local communities and pilgrims.
- A setting for Newar cultural practices and annual festivities.
- A public park and heritage landscape.

Possible issues to investigate include water flow and quality, maintenance of stone structures, waste management during festivals, visitor pressure, accessibility, and continuity of local rituals. These are research questions, not confirmed claims about current conditions.

2. PYAH THWAM AJIMA / PYATHO AJIMA / SHITALA MAI
============================================================

IDENTITY AND LOCATION
Pyah Thwam Ajima (प्याः थ्वँ अजिमा), also written in some sources as Pyatho Ajima, is described in local reporting as a shrine within the historic Balaju Baisdhara Park complex. The deity is also locally referred to as Shitala Mai.

Ajima broadly refers to a divine or protective mother figure in Newar religious tradition. Academic research explains that Ajima/Matrika goddesses are worshipped by different Newar communities in both Hindu and Buddhist settings, and that their shrines and forms vary across the Kathmandu Valley.

Caution: the academic source listed below provides general context about Ajima worship. It is not a site-specific historical study of the Pyah Thwam Ajima shrine.

RELIGIOUS SIGNIFICANCE AND BELIEFS
Local reporting associates Pyah Thwam Ajima with protection from illness, epidemics, and threats to children's wellbeing. The wider Hindu tradition of Shitala is also associated with protection from diseases such as smallpox and fever.

These are religious and cultural beliefs, not scientifically proven methods of preventing or treating disease.

LOCAL NAME AND ORAL TRADITION
A local article offers an interpretation of the name:
- “Pyah” is interpreted as thirst.
- “Thwam” is associated with traditional Newar rice beer.

The article links this interpretation to an oral story involving thirst, drought or water shortage, and epidemics. However, the article itself notes that clear ancient written evidence for this etymology and origin story is lacking.

Present this as a local interpretation or oral tradition, not as a verified linguistic or historical fact. The meaning should be checked with knowledgeable local Newar speakers, ritual practitioners, and Guthi members.

DRISTI LEKHAN: THE EYE-PAINTING RITUAL
One distinctive tradition described in local reporting is Dristi Lekhan. During the Lhuti Purnima observance, members of the Pũ (Chitrakar) community's Guthi traditionally paint the eyes and eyebrows of the goddess image.

The article describes this as a religiously meaningful ritual rather than ordinary decoration. It also suggests that public viewing or access to the image may be restricted until the ritual is completed.

This practice matters for heritage documentation because it involves:
- Traditional artistic knowledge.
- A community-specific ritual role.
- Intergenerational transmission of skills.
- Rules about when and how the deity image may be viewed.

The exact procedure, terminology, timing, and restrictions should be confirmed directly with participating artists and the Guthi. Do not assume the full ritual is open to photography or public observation.

OFFERINGS AND WORSHIP
The local article describes offerings that may include Thwam, Aila, abir, sindoor, flowers, incense, and Samay Baji items such as beaten rice, roasted soybeans, choila, and bara.

It also mentions an animal-sacrifice tradition and alternatives such as eggplant or ash gourd for worshippers who do not offer an animal. Treat these as the article's account of local practice, not as a universal rule followed by every devotee.

Confirm which offerings are used, by whom, on which occasions, and whether practices differ among families or Guthi groups.

CONNECTION WITH 22 DHARA AND LHUTI PUNHI
The shrine is connected with the wider Baisdhara/Lhuti Punhi observance. Local reporting describes devotees bathing at the 22 Dhara spouts and then worshipping Pyah Thwam Ajima.

This connection shows that Balaju's heritage is not limited to its stone water spouts. It includes the relationship between water, deity worship, seasonal festivals, community participation, traditional art, and Guthi responsibilities.

GUTHI AND LIVING HERITAGE
Local reporting highlights the role of Guthi institutions in maintaining rituals, community responsibilities, traditional skills, and festival continuity.

The same article raises concerns such as Guthi land encroachment, management difficulties, administrative complexity, and challenges in passing traditions to younger generations. These are claims made by that article and have not been independently verified in the sources reviewed.

Useful questions for local consultation:
- Who is responsible for the shrine and its rituals?
- How are ritual duties assigned?
- How is knowledge passed to younger members?
- What changes have occurred in recent years?
- What support is needed to preserve the tradition?

============================================================
3. HOW THE TWO HERITAGE SITES ARE CONNECTED
============================================================

22 Dhara and Pyah Thwam Ajima should not be researched as unrelated monuments.

22 Dhara represents the water and public-infrastructure dimension of the Balaju heritage landscape. Pyah Thwam Ajima represents the deity-worship, Newar ritual knowledge, traditional art, and community-institution dimension.

Their connection through Lhuti Punhi/Baisdhara observances illustrates living heritage: a place remains culturally meaningful not only because of its physical structures, but also because people continue to use it, maintain it, worship there, and transmit its practices.

COMPARISON
22 DHARA
- Main feature: 22 stone water spouts.
- Heritage themes: water infrastructure, ritual bathing, public space, annual festival.
- Community dimension: devotees, local residents, festival organizers, and related religious groups.
- Research questions: water source and quality, structure maintenance, festival date, visitor management.

PYAH THWAM AJIMA
- Main feature: a local Ajima/Shitala Mai shrine.
- Heritage themes: Newar religious tradition, protective-deity beliefs, Dristi Lekhan, offerings, Guthi responsibilities.
- Community dimension: devotees, Guthi members, Pũ/Chitrakar traditional artists, and the local Newar community.
- Research questions: ritual details, oral history, artist roles, Guthi management, and transmission to younger generations.

4. POSSIBLE AI WORKSHOP IDEAS
============================================================

These are concepts, not confirmed community requirements. Develop them only after consulting residents, Guthi members, artists, and heritage managers.

IDEA A: DIGITAL LIVING-HERITAGE ARCHIVE
Create a searchable archive containing:
- Verified site history and source references.
- Audio interviews with local residents, Guthi members, priests, and artists.
- Photos and descriptions of rituals, where recording is permitted.
- Newari and Nepali terms with explanations.
- Festival calendars and changes over time.
- Clear labels distinguishing documented facts from oral traditions.

AI could help transcribe interviews, translate between Nepali/Newari/English, generate searchable tags, and answer questions from the verified archive. Human review is essential, especially for religious terminology and oral histories.

IDEA B: HERITAGE WATER MONITORING
For 22 Dhara, a monitoring system could record:
- Whether water is flowing from each spout.
- Basic water-quality readings collected by qualified people.
- Visible damage or blockages.
- Maintenance reports and dates.
- Changes during festival periods.

AI could identify patterns or flag unusual changes in collected data. AI alone cannot determine whether water is safe; laboratory testing and qualified assessment are required.

IDEA C: COMMUNITY-LED RITUAL AND FESTIVAL DOCUMENTATION
Build a platform where authorized community representatives can document festival dates, ritual responsibilities, traditional art practices, changes in procedures, oral histories, and community concerns.

Access controls are important. Some ritual knowledge may be private or restricted. The community must decide what can be recorded and shared publicly.

IDEA D: RESPONSIBLE HERITAGE INFORMATION ASSISTANT
Create a multilingual assistant that answers visitor questions using only a verified, locally approved knowledge base. It should cite sources for historical claims, label oral traditions and religious beliefs, avoid presenting beliefs as medical facts, explain visitor etiquette and photography restrictions, and admit when information is uncertain.

============================================================
5. RESEARCH LIMITATIONS: WHAT MUST BE VERIFIED
============================================================

1. The royal construction history of the 22 spouts is widely reported, but primary evidence was not established in the sources reviewed.
2. Published sources disagree about the exact date of the annual Baisdhara/Lhuti Punhi event. Confirm with organizers for the relevant year.
3. Claims that the water cures or prevents skin diseases are religious/traditional beliefs, not medical evidence.
4. The etymology and drought-related origin story of Pyah Thwam Ajima are presented as local tradition; ancient written evidence was not established.
5. The detailed Dristi Lekhan account comes from local journalism and should be corroborated with the Pũ/Chitrakar Guthi and traditional artists.
6. Offerings and sacrifice practices may vary. Do not generalize them to all worshippers.
7. The current condition of the water system, water quality, accessibility, and conservation needs require current on-site or official verification.
8. Do not record or publish restricted ritual information without permission from the relevant community.

7. SHORT PRESENTATION SUMMARY
============================================================

22 Dhara, also known as Baisdhara, is a historic water-spout complex in Balaju with 22 stone spouts. It is associated with traditional water infrastructure, ritual bathing, and the annual Baisdhara/Lhuti Punhi gathering. The commonly reported history attributes 21 spouts to King Jaya Prakash Malla and one to a later ruler, but this history should be presented with a source caveat. Festival-date reporting is inconsistent and should be confirmed with local organizers.

Pyah Thwam Ajima, also called Pyatho Ajima or Shitala Mai, is a local protective mother-deity shrine within the Balaju heritage landscape. Local reporting describes its connection with Newar religious beliefs, children's wellbeing, illness-protection beliefs, offerings, the Dristi Lekhan eye-painting ritual performed by the Pũ/Chitrakar community, and Guthi responsibilities. Some details are oral traditions and need confirmation from the community.

Together, the two sites show that Balaju's heritage is both tangible and intangible: stone water infrastructure and shrines, as well as rituals, festivals, traditional art, local knowledge, and community institutions.
